---
title: Preliminary vote count: what it is and how technology speeds it up
---
The preliminary count is one of the most visible stages of an electoral process. It is the moment when citizens expect to learn the results, the media broadcast data in real time and political actors begin to interpret trends. Its speed and accuracy largely determine public perception of the election's transparency.

However, in many jurisdictions the preliminary count remains an operational bottleneck. Delays in publishing results, transcription errors and the lack of traceability in the data chain generate uncertainty and, in some cases, avoidable crises of confidence.

This article explains what the preliminary count is, how it differs from the final count, what challenges it faces in its manual version and how technology can speed it up without compromising oversight or public control of the process.

## What is the preliminary count

The preliminary count is the initial tally of the votes cast in an election, carried out as quickly as possible after polls close.

Its purpose is to inform citizens about result trends within a reasonable timeframe, reducing the uncertainty that arises between the closing of the polls and the official proclamation.

### Characteristics of the preliminary count

The preliminary count has traits that distinguish it from other instances of the electoral process. Its results are informational and non-binding: they have no legal validity for proclaiming winners.

The priority is processing speed to achieve early dissemination of trends. And although it is a preliminary tally, it must be a clear, auditable process that generates confidence in the data being communicated.

### Difference from the final count

The final count is the official tally carried out by the competent electoral authority once the day is over. It includes the formal review of tally sheets, the resolution of challenges and observations, and the legal proclamation of results. Its timeframe is longer because it prioritizes thoroughness over speed.

The preliminary and final counts serve complementary functions. The preliminary count responds to the immediate need for information.

The final count grants legal validity. Both must be accurate, but the preliminary count operates under time pressure that makes it especially vulnerable to operational problems.

## The challenges of the manual preliminary count

When the preliminary count is managed entirely manually, the data chain between the polling station and the publication of results faces several friction points.

### Transcription and transport of tally sheets

Once the polling station authority completes the tally sheet, the physical document must be transported to a computing center where the data is entered into the system.

This transport introduces delays that vary according to distance, available logistics and conditions on election day.

In large jurisdictions or those with limited road infrastructure, tally sheets from the most remote stations can take hours to arrive.

### Manual data entry

At the computing center, data entry operators transcribe the data from each physical tally sheet into the computer system. This process is slow and error-prone: a misread number, data entered in the wrong category or a sum that does not add up.

Each error requires a review that consumes additional time and can generate inconsistencies in the partial results being published.

### Centralization and bottlenecks

Depending on a computing center as the single point of data entry creates congestion. Tally sheets arrive at uneven rates, operators process at limited speed and any incident at that facility affects the entire process.

If the computing center becomes saturated or experiences a technical failure, the publication of results stops.

### Traceability gap

In the manual model, tally sheet data passes through multiple hands between the polling station and the computing center. Party overseers may be present at the station during the count, but they generally do not accompany the tally sheet during transport or data entry.

This gap opens a space where traceability weakens and suspicion can thrive, even when no real irregularities exist.

### Impact on public trust

Prolonged delays in publishing preliminary results create an information vacuum that is filled with speculation, rumors and accusations, often unfounded.

The experience of multiple jurisdictions shows that the speed of the preliminary count has a direct correlation with public trust in the transparency of the process.

## How technology speeds up the preliminary count

Modernizing the preliminary count centers on a concrete operating principle: shortening the data chain between the polling station and the publication of results, eliminating intermediaries and maintaining oversight at every step.

### Counting at the station

The starting point does not change. At the end of the day, polling station authorities count the votes manually, as required by regulations, and record the results on the tally sheet.

Party overseers participate in this process and sign the sheet. This step is identical to the traditional model.

### Digitization at the polling place

Instead of transporting the physical tally sheet to a computing center, the sheet is scanned directly at the same venue where voting took place. This eliminates transport as a source of delays and as a blind spot in the traceability chain.

### Assisted interpretation and validation

The system performs an initial interpretation of the scanned tally sheet data. This interpretation is presented to the polling station authority, who validates it in the presence of party overseers.

Human validation at the venue is the mechanism that ensures the digital data faithfully matches the physical tally sheet. Control of the process remains in the hands of electoral actors, not the computer system.

### Secure transmission

Once validated, the digitized information is transmitted using encrypted protocols such as TLS to a computing center for consolidation.

Operator authentication ensures that only authorized personnel can send data, and encryption protects the integrity of the information during transmission.

### Periodic publication of results

At the computing center, the received data goes through a quality control flow before being incorporated into the results.

Publication is periodic and automated, with intervals that can be as frequent as every 15 minutes, allowing citizens to access updated results as tally sheets are processed.

### Contingency management

A critical aspect of any preliminary count system is its behavior in the face of connectivity problems.

A well-designed system ensures that if no connection is available at the venue, the data is stored securely on the device and transmitted as soon as the connection is restored, or from an authorized alternative transmission point. No tally sheet is lost due to infrastructure problems.

## What electoral authorities should evaluate

The decision to modernize the preliminary count requires considering factors that go beyond technology.

The legal framework of each jurisdiction defines the requirements the preliminary count must meet, the publication deadlines and the oversight conditions. The technological solution must operate within that framework.

Training polling station authorities is essential, since they are the ones who validate the digitized data and give it legitimacy before the overseers.

Compatibility with the existing voting method is a relevant practical factor: the preliminary count can be digitized regardless of whether the election uses electronic ballots or manual voting, allowing this stage to be modernized without changing the voting system.

The preliminary count is the stage of the electoral process where citizens form their first impression of the election's transparency.

Modernizing it with technology that combines speed, accuracy and public oversight is one of the investments with the greatest return in institutional trust an electoral authority can make.
